What a wash-aisle dispatcher and a Kubernetes scheduler have in common

Notes from a talk on plant-floor orchestration, given to a room of people who think in containers, not carts. Edited for reading; the jokes that died are omitted.

Naveed Kakal November 2025 Orchestration · systems

I want to convince you of something slightly ridiculous: that the most instructive distributed-systems deployment you could study is not running in a data center. It is a building on the edge of your city that washes hotel sheets, and the scheduler is a person named Doris.

Here is the setup. An industrial laundry is, structurally, a cluster. The washers, dryers, presses, and ironers are nodes with wildly heterogeneous capacity. The work arrives as batches — a cart of bar mops, a sling of surgical gowns — each with a workload spec: a wash formula, a finishing route, a customer deadline. Between the nodes run conveyors and overhead monorails, which are the network, complete with congestion, dropped packets (a sling that mis-routes), and a hard global constraint that data-center people never face: the packets weigh a hundred pounds and two of them cannot occupy the same rail at the same time.

And in the middle of it stands the wash-aisle dispatcher, deciding — continuously, all shift — which batch goes into which machine next. After two decades of building software to assist, and partially replace, that person, I can report: she is doing bin-packing with affinity rules, preemption, and backpressure, in her head, at 95 decibels. The comparison to a Kubernetes scheduler is not a metaphor. It is nearly an isomorphism. Let me walk the mapping, because the places where it holds — and the two places it breaks — both teach you something.

The mapping holds better than it should

Affinity and taints. A surgical-linen batch can only run on machines validated for healthcare work — node affinity. The press that is running hot today should get nothing delicate — a taint, applied by a maintenance tech with a grease pencil instead of kubectl. Doris tracks a dozen of these constraints, and the most important ones are not in any system: she knows machine four lies about its temperature.

Bin-packing against the expensive resource. A tunnel washer is a 16-pocket pipeline, and the scarce resource is not pocket count, it is thermal continuity — alternating hot and cold formulas wastes energy and time, so good dispatchers batch by temperature class the way a good scheduler packs by GPU. Same gradient, different physics.

Backpressure. When the dryers fall behind, the washer must slow down, because the buffer between them is a physical floor that fills with wet linen. Wet linen cannot wait the way a queue waits — it wrinkles, it mildews, it has an SLA with chemistry. A dispatcher reads buffer state at a glance and throttles upstream. Every plant that has ever ignored backpressure has learned what an unbounded queue looks like when it is made of towels.

Preemption. The hospital truck leaves at 2 p.m. whether or not the gowns are on it. As the deadline approaches, the dispatcher will pull lower-priority work out of the finishing line mid-stream — pod eviction, except the evicted pod is physically pushed to a corner and somebody has to remember it exists. Which brings us to the first place the mapping breaks.

Break one: there is no kill and reschedule

In Kubernetes, the scheduler's superpower is that work is restartable. Kill the pod, run it elsewhere, idempotency absorbs the violence. On a laundry floor, nothing is idempotent. A batch that is eight minutes into a wash formula cannot be cancelled and resubmitted; it is wet, partially processed state that must be carried forward. Every scheduling decision is a one-way door taken under uncertainty.

This changes the optimal strategy profoundly. Restartable-work schedulers can be greedy and correct themselves later. Irreversible-work schedulers must keep slack — and watching expert dispatchers, that is exactly what they do. They run the plant at a deliberate distance from saturation, holding capacity in reserve, scheduling later than software designers expect, because a deferred decision is the only kind they can still get right. When we built Autopilot, our automated soil-side routing, the single biggest improvement came not from a cleverer packing algorithm but from teaching it to wait: to refuse to commit a batch until the last responsible moment. Decision latency, it turns out, is a resource, and humans who do this job spend it brilliantly.

Break two: the scheduler is also the sensor network

A Kubernetes scheduler trusts its inputs — kubelet says the node is ready, so the node is ready. Doris trusts nothing. She schedules with her ears. A bearing that has started to sing, a press that sounds wrong on the down-stroke, a crew on station three that is half a step slow because it is Friday — she folds all of it into placement decisions as naturally as CPU requests. Her telemetry is richer than ours, even after we instrumented everything from the breaker panel up.

The dispatcher is not just executing a scheduling policy. She is continuously re-estimating the truthfulness of every signal the plant emits — and the formal system, by definition, only sees the signals.

This is the deep reason "lights-out" plant automation keeps underdelivering, in laundry and elsewhere. It is not that the optimization is hard — it is that the human being replaced was quietly doing sensor fusion, anomaly detection, and workforce psychology in addition to the scheduling you noticed. Automate the visible function and the invisible ones fall on the floor, sometimes literally.

What each side should steal

If you build infrastructure software: study operators like Doris before you replace them, because the gap between the job description and the job is where your outage lives. Treat irreversibility as a first-class property of work, not an inconvenience — more of your workloads are one-way doors than your retry logic admits. And respect deferred commitment as a strategy; the best schedulers, silicon or human, are distinguished less by how well they decide than by how long they responsibly avoid deciding.

And if you run a plant: the container people have spent fifteen years writing down, in public, battle-tested vocabulary for exactly the problems your floor solves by oral tradition — backpressure, affinity, preemption, priority classes. The concepts port. The vocabulary alone is worth the trip, because once the wash aisle and the front office share words for these things, they can finally argue about the right ones.

Doris, for the record, was unimpressed when I explained that she was a scheduler. She pointed out that she also fixes the scheduler when it breaks. She is, of course, correct — and that is a talk for next year. ❦
