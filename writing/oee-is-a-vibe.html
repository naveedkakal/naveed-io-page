OEE is a vibe, not a metric

What ten years of measuring overall equipment effectiveness taught me about the gap between dashboards and decisions.

Naveed Kakal February 2026 Telemetry · operations

Overall equipment effectiveness is the metric everyone in industrial software eventually builds. The formula is seductive in its tidiness: availability × performance × quality, one number between zero and one hundred, the whole truth of a machine. We have computed it across hundreds of plants for over a decade. Here is the uncomfortable conclusion: as a number, OEE is nearly useless. As a vibe — a shared, directionally honest feeling about how the plant is doing — it is one of the most valuable things you can put on a wall.

That distinction sounds flippant. It is the most practical thing I know about plant telemetry.

The number lies in three specific ways

First, every term in the formula is a negotiation. Availability requires deciding what counts as planned downtime. Is the twenty-minute changeover between hotel sheets and surgical gowns a planned event or a loss? The textbook has an answer; the plant has an argument. Performance requires an ideal cycle rate, and "ideal" for an ironer depends on the fabric, the moisture retention coming out of the dryers, even the weather, because humidity changes how linen behaves. Pick the nameplate rating and every plant runs at 60% forever. Pick a "realistic" rate and you have just encoded your current performance as the ceiling. Either way, the number now measures your assumptions, not your machine.

Second, the aggregation destroys the information you actually need. 75% OEE could mean a reliable machine running slightly slow, or a fast machine that died for two hours, or a quality problem rewashing one batch in eight. Those three plants need three completely different interventions — a tuning conversation, a maintenance conversation, a wash-chemistry conversation. The single number is constructed precisely by multiplying away the distinction. It answers "how are we doing?" by destroying the answer to "what should we do?"

Third, the moment OEE becomes a target, it gets gamed — Goodhart's law, with forklifts. Reclassify downtime as planned, and availability rises. Quietly lower the ideal rate at the start of the quarter, and performance rises. I have watched a plant "improve" OEE nine points in a quarter without a single pound of additional linen leaving the building. Nobody was being dishonest, exactly. The metric simply rewards redefinition more cheaply than it rewards improvement, and organizations follow gradients.

And yet I keep shipping it

Here is the twist that took me years to make peace with: knowing all of the above, our software still computes and displays OEE, prominently. Because the failure modes above are failures of OEE as a precision instrument — comparing plants against each other, setting bonus targets, making capital decisions off a third decimal place. Used instead as a trend line about itself, with its definitions frozen and its components one tap away, it does something subtle and important: it gives a hot, loud, fragmented operation a shared object to point at.

The value of the number on the wall is not that it is correct. It is that the wash-aisle lead, the maintenance tech, and the GM are all looking at the same wall.

When the line dips, the argument that follows — was that the changeover? the boiler? the new crew on shift two? — is exactly the conversation the plant needed to have, and would not have had without the dip. The metric is a conversation-starting device wearing the costume of a measurement. Most operational dashboards, if we are honest, work this way. The ones that fail are the ones that take their own precision seriously.

What this implies if you build telemetry

  • Freeze the definitions and version them. An OEE figure is meaningless without its definition set, so treat changeover rules and ideal rates like schema migrations: dated, logged, and visible. The trend is the product; protect its continuity.
  • Never show the composite without one-tap decomposition. The number invites the question "why?" — answering it must cost one gesture, or you have built a frustration machine.
  • Trend within a plant; never league-table across plants. Cross-plant OEE comparisons are comparisons of negotiation outcomes. The healthy comparison is a plant against its own last month.
  • Keep it off the bonus sheet. The day OEE prices a paycheck is the day it stops measuring the plant and starts measuring creativity. Pay on things that leave on trucks.
  • Optimize for the argument, not the audit. If the metric reliably causes the right three people to stand in front of the right machine, it is working — whatever the auditors think of the methodology.

The general lesson outlasts the laundry industry. Every composite metric — OEE, NPS, a sprint velocity, an engineering "DORA score" — is a vibe wearing a number's clothing. That is not a reason to discard them. It is a reason to deploy them as what they are: instruments for synchronizing attention, not instruments for measuring truth. Confuse the two and you get gaming and theater. Keep them straight and a slightly wrong number, watched together, will outperform a perfect number watched by no one. ❦
