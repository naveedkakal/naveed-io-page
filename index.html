§ 01 — the story
2006 → now

Twenty years in one industry, by choice. I make laundry plants legible to the people who run them.

Before laundry, there was a car wash — nine years at Royal Oaks in Shoreview, Minnesota, learning how chemistry, machines and a line of customers share one schedule. In August 2006 ETECH hired me on a hunch that car washes have spiritual similarities to laundry controls. The hunch held.

An industrial laundry happens in three dimensions: bags of linen riding rails overhead, a sort below, machines that never stop. The software's job is to keep an honest picture of all of it — and to help people fix the picture when it lies. I've spent my career on that picture: the machine comms, the plant state, the tools the floor uses to set things right.

Today I'm Director of Software Development at Kannegiesser ETECH, seeing several hundred plants through their daily problems and growth ideas. I'm an IC at heart, so the job is mostly judgment: what's worth building, what survives contact with the floor, and what a fleet of AI agents should do with twenty years of pattern-matching.

§ 02 — field rules
learned the hard way

What twenty years on the floor taught me.

  1. R-01

    Count problems solved per developer dollar.

    Well-run teams can still produce a flotilla of blah. The ledger that matters is what the floor stopped suffering.

    from · The prank that brought him back
  2. R-02

    Remove the seams.

    Responsibility leaks out through the joints between specialists. Fewer hand-offs, fewer places for the obvious fix to die.

    from · Seventeen years in the same problem
  3. R-03

    Speak the floor's language.

    Sling. Rail. Batch. When the software uses the floor's words, the people on the floor can tell when it's wrong — and help it get right.

    from · Seventeen years in the same problem
  4. R-04

    Let reality win.

    The plant and its picture will drift. When they do, ask a human what actually happened, and offer only answers that can physically occur.

    from · Seventeen years in the same problem
  5. R-05

    Trust is the infrastructure.

    If they trust you, they'll leave you alone to fix it right. If they don't, you'll never have a peaceful project. Showing up, coffee in hand, is a deliverable.

    from · Teaching the condemned new tricks
  6. R-06

    Improve the condemned.

    Push a dying system as far as it will go. Its real limits — not the rumored ones — become the spec for whatever replaces it.

    from · Teaching the condemned new tricks
→ where this is going

One person who knows the floor. A fleet of agents that knows the code. I think that's the next shape of a software team — and I've been rehearsing for it for twenty years.
