§ 01 — the stack, bottom up
six layers · one person · since 2006

Every layer of a plant. I've written code in all of them.

Most software careers live on one floor of the building. Mine takes the stairs: from the bit a sensor flips to the alert on a supervisor's phone.

L1 I/O · the floor

Everything starts as a bit.

A photo-eye sees a bag go by. A load cell weighs it, and in this business weight is money. A drive spins a lift. Bus couplers turn hundreds of these into network traffic: one plant I support runs 132 networked I/O panels.

In my second week on the record, March 2009, I started the habit that became our testing standard: click to force. Click any sensor in the software and the plant acts as if a real bag had tripped it. Tap a ◆ on the rail to try it.

L2 machine control

I don't hand-write the PLC programs. I generate them.

Each plant's database describes its rails, lifts and panels. A generator I maintain turns that into the B&R PLC program: 129 device types, 123 PLCs in the fleet, and a hash check that proves a change didn't move a byte it shouldn't.

Talking to the PLC has had three lives: SOAP from Rails in 2007, an Elixir service I started in 2019, an OPC UA bridge today. One rule survived all three. Never act on a stale sensor. Read it again, live, before releasing anything.

L3 screens & workflows

Then a person has to run it.

In 2007 the operator screens were a per-seat license. The floor wanted eyes everywhere; the invoice said pick two. So we made the screen a web page.

That became eVue: routing over the rail graph, storage lanes, sort stations, operator logins by task, an autopilot that decides which batch goes next, and alarms that say in plain words why a bag isn't moving. Today it runs on Rails 8 and React 19, brought forward plant by plant, never rewritten.

L4 local reporting

The plant reads its own numbers.

Every rail visit, machine visit and login is a row, so the plant can answer for itself: throughput by the hour, dryer time, sort use, and operator productivity that's fair to the operator. Plus a 6 a.m. report email someone actually opens.

All of it lives on the plant's own server. When the internet goes down, the floor doesn't notice.

L5 cloud reporting

Then the company reads all of them.

Each plant runs on its own. A hub at every site rolls its numbers up to the cloud every minute, so the people running a company of plants see one picture instead of forty phone calls.

The fleet is the hard part: 400+ plants, each its own building, floor plan and set of machines. One afternoon this fall, AI agents scanned ~300 plant repos for one rename and opened 86 pull requests. 42 landed that day; 14 were held on purpose.

L6 mobile

And finally, a phone in a pocket.

Plant networks are walled off, and they should be. Pulse is how the plant's data gets out anyway, securely: a supervisor links a phone by scanning a QR code at the plant, then gets the numbers that matter, and a push when something is really wrong.

It's the top of the stack, and the part people actually carry. That's the whole building, bottom to top.

§ 03 — the boring superpower

I don't leave. I take care of things.

Before laundry there was a car wash: nine years at Royal Oaks in Shoreview, Minnesota, ending as the manager. In 2006 an industrial controls company hired me, fresh out of electrical engineering, on a hunch that car washes and laundries have a lot in common. They do. Machines, water, valuable things moving through a tunnel on a schedule, and nobody can stop the line.

Today I'm Lead Developer at Kannegiesser ETECH. The building can't stop, so the software can't either, and I'm still the one who gets on the plane.

field rules — how I design, learned the hard way
R-01 · learned at Centro

Count problems solved per developer dollar.

Well-run teams can still produce a flotilla of blah. The ledger that matters is what the floor stopped suffering.

R-02

Remove the seams.

Responsibility leaks out through the joints between specialists. I'd rather own the stack top to bottom than hand off at every floor.

R-03

Speak the floor's language.

Sling, rail, batch. When the software uses the floor's own words, the floor can tell when the software is wrong.

R-04

Let reality win.

When the plant and its digital picture disagree, ask a human what happened, and offer only answers that can physically occur.

R-05

Chase the bottleneck, not the fashion.

I moved my analytics service from Rails to Rust in a day. I kept the PLC bridge in Node: the PLC is the slow part, and a rewrite would reopen nine solved production bugs.

R-06

Improve the condemned.

Push a dying system as far as it will go. Its real limits, not the rumored ones, become the spec for whatever replaces it.

§ 04 — the night shift
Oct 2025 → Oct 2026

Then AI showed up.

It started two years ago, copy-pasting from ChatGPT. This year it became a fleet. The AI I care about most is still for the people on the floor.

Weave · AI maintenance for laundries · in production

A tech scans the machine. The machine tells them how to fix it.

A maintenance tech is responsible for dozens of machine types, and half get serviced less than once a year. Nobody remembers the safe procedure for an annual deep clean. In Weave, they scan a QR code on the machine and get it, step by step, written from the manufacturer's manual with the page cited, in plain English (Spanish when asked). The crew already talks in a group text, so Weave joined the group text. The owner gets a plain-English report every Monday.

One person who knows the floor. A fleet of agents that knows the code. I decide what ships.

Since July, most of it runs through Burrow, a software factory I wrote from scratch: agents and autonomous workflows that run in production on my own machines, 1,436 runs across 27 feeds so far. Work comes in from tickets, cards and timers, walks a path I drew, and comes back done, or with a question only I can answer. A model from a different vendor reviews every change it writes. When it isn't sure, it parks. It never guesses.

That's how a bit more effort turns an idea into something that runs every week: a Monday industry digest, a nightly product review, a science channel. Every run is costed.
